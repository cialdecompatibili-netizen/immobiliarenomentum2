Before viewing a home it is worth knowing how much you can spend. Searching in the wrong price range wastes time and, in the worst case, leads you to sign a preliminary contract with a loan the bank then refuses to grant. Here is where to start.

## The deposit and the purchase costs

In many cases the bank finances a share of the property value, often up to 80%, and the rest is paid by the buyer. On top of this come the purchase costs: taxes, the notary's fee, any agency commission, application and valuation fees. These costs must be taken into account from the start, and their amount depends on the case: it should be checked with the notary and the bank.

## The instalment you can afford

A common benchmark is not to exceed about a third of your monthly net income, but this is not a legal rule: it depends on your other expenses, your family and how stable your job is. The bank assesses your income, the type of contract and your existing commitments, such as other loans or instalments.

## Fixed, variable or mixed rate

- **Fixed**: the instalment stays the same for the whole term, useful if you want certainty.
- **Variable**: it follows interest rates and can go up or down.
- **Mixed**: it allows you to change formula over time according to the conditions set out in the contract.

To compare offers look at the APR (TAEG), which includes the costs as well as the rate, and not only at the nominal rate (TAN).

## The documents the bank asks for

You generally need an identity document, tax code, payslips or tax returns, an employment contract and information about the property. The bank then has a valuation carried out to estimate the value: if the estimate is lower than the agreed price, the amount financed may be reduced.

## The practical advice

Run an affordability check before you start viewing, and set a realistic price ceiling. With a clear budget you can move with confidence, and the seller takes you seriously.
